What this map is, and what it is not

What it is

  • A map of attention. Green shows how much sustained news coverage a place gets. Pale means quiet, dark means active.
  • A map of blind spots. Orange hatching marks places where conditions are serious and coverage is thin. Bolder lines mean a larger gap.
  • One method for every shape on the map, including small states, territories and Palestine. Coverage is scored 0 to 100. Severity adds five parts, each 0 to 20: violence and conflict, humanitarian need, rights and governance, economic stress, and direction of travel. Urgency is severity, raised when coverage falls short of it. Hatching starts at 60.
  • Refreshed every 30 minutes. A job reads the GDELT global news feed, counts mentions and conflict-coded events per country over 24 hours, and moves the scores a limited amount from their baseline.

What it is not

  • A ranking of good and bad countries. A pale country is quiet in the news. Quiet says nothing about whether it is well governed.
  • A forecast. It does not predict war, famine or collapse.
  • A source of facts. Scores are editorial judgments. The facts, with dates and links, sit in each country report.
  • Finished. Five countries have sourced reports (Sudan, Congo, Haiti, Myanmar, Yemen). Every other score is preliminary, drafted by AI from general knowledge and not yet sourced or reviewed by a person. Treat it as provisional until its report is published.
  • A clean measure of attention. GDELT leans toward English-language and wire coverage, so local press is undercounted.

How the score works

Each issue is scored on five tests worth up to 20 points each: great-power stake, economic reach (energy, minerals, trade routes), spillover risk (other states drawn in), human reach (displacement, famine, migration beyond borders) and order and precedent (alliances, nuclear and legal norms).

The score measures what is happening now, not what could happen. Every test links to its sources, and each entry shows the newest source date. Scores are editorial judgments drafted by AI and held for editor approval.

Method: three lenses on every issue
i.

Local

What people living through an event say about it: civil society, regional outlets, diaspora voices, community networks. Often missing from international coverage.

ii.

International

How wire services, governments and global media frame the same event, and where those framings diverge from what is happening on the ground.

iii.

Scholar

Historical context, key actors, timelines and long-term implications from researchers and specialist institutions.
